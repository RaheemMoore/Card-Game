The Debt-Bearer's arena — three directions

Each shown with the real boss sprite at his actual in-game scale and position, and with the interface regions marked, so these are judged as arenas rather than as pictures.

Blue boxes = boss HUD and combat journal panels. Yellow lines = the band the dais top must land in. Black bar = covered by the command shelf.

A — The Forge-Hall of Debts

Furnace banks glowing through their grates, hanging iron counting-weights on chains, molten channels ringing a black iron dais.

My read: Most on-theme: it reads as a place where iron is heated and debts are tallied. Darkest of the three, so the boss has least separation from it.

B — The Counting Floor

A cold hall of pale tally-stone, dark upper corners, clean uncluttered floor.

My read: Best composition compliance and by far the best contrast — cold blue stone against a fire boss makes him pop. But the carved tally notches and fire seams did not come through, so it is the least characterful.

C — The Cooling Yard

Open air under a red-black sky, iron gantries, a wall of fire, cracked ash floor.

My read: Most dramatic by a distance. But the bright red sky sits exactly where the boss HUD and journal panels go, which breaks the dark-corner rule — fixable with a re-roll, not with code.

How these were made pixel art

Phoenix will not produce pixel art on request — with Alchemy on it returns a 3D render, with it off a smooth painting. Neither belongs behind a pixel sprite. So the pixelisation is now a deterministic post-process: box-downsample to a 453x256 native grid, reduce to a limited palette, scale back with nearest-neighbour. It costs nothing, always gives the same result, and lets the grid be matched to the sprite instead of hoped for.

One tuning note worth keeping: the palette reduction has to be given a saturation boost first. These plates are overwhelmingly dark stone, so the few hot pixels get merged toward white and the lava turns cream — which is exactly what the first pass did.

Then we liven it up in code

Once you pick one, the motion is free and needs no further art: drifting embers, heat shimmer over the molten channels, a slow pulse on the furnace glow, and the chains and hanging weights swaying. That is CSS and canvas, not generations — the same approach the weapon ring already uses.
